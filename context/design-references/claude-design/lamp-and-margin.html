1 · Human writing

Your words hang into the margin

Decision. A human paragraph breaks the column: it outdents into the left margin behind a 3px warm rule, set in the reading face, in cream on dark. The paper stays in the column. AI stays inset on a surface. Three positions, no labels.

Ink warmth alone was a 1.03:1 difference, invisible by construction. Position is the strongest cue the eye has, and marginalia is the oldest convention for "a reader wrote here." The rule is the same object in both themes, lit on dark and pigment on light, so the grammar is one sentence: a warm line in the margin means you wrote this.

2 · Accent

Warm is what you made. Cold is what you can do.

Decision. Two hues, two meanings, never on the same pixel. Warm belongs to content the human produced: the reacted passage, the margin rule, the lamp. Cold belongs to the interface offering itself: hover, active, the citation under the cursor, the frontier line on the map. The cold is a low-chroma teal at hue 198°, not mint.

Your objection is the correct one and it is color theory, not preference. A hue carries meaning only by contrast with another hue. When the wash, the handwriting, the lamp, and the hover all sit in one warm family, the eye reads "warm" once and stops. Nothing in the chrome can announce itself, and the product goes flat exactly where you want it to feel alive. The 1b revision's mistake was not adding a second hue; it was the hue's coordinates.

Why 198° and why this chroma:

  • Opposition, not complement. The warm run centers near 55°. Its exact complement is 235°, which is blue, which you rejected. At 198° the cold sits far enough across the wheel to separate cleanly from every warm in the system while staying out of blue.
  • Lightness sets the hierarchy. Verdigris at lightness .84 sat almost level with the paper's ink at .94, so controls competed with content. The cold is set at .78 on dark, always beneath the reading ink and above the secondary ink. On light it never appears as text at all; see section 7. Content stays the brightest thing in the room.
  • Chroma separates ink from glow. Teal at high lightness drifts green and reads as mint, the Bezold-Brücke shift. Lower lightness pulls it back toward petrol. Chroma .075 on dark and .085 on light is enough to be unmistakably a color and not enough to fluoresce.
  • It is native to basalt. The ground is 248°. A 198° accent is analogous to it, fifty degrees away, so on dark the cold feels like it belongs to the room and the warm lamp is the visitor. On warm paper at 91° the same hue is near-complementary, so it carries more weight there with less chroma, which is why the light-mode wash drops to chroma .04 and the light-mode line to lightness .60.

3 · The lamp

Light adds. Paint mixes.

Decision. The dark-mode glow is amber, hue near 70°, composited with a screen blend rather than alpha. The pastel run is rebuilt at equal lightness and chroma. Basalt stays cool on purpose.

Three pieces of color science explain why the current lamp is gray and why the earlier one was khaki.

  • Yellow has no dark. Every hue except yellow keeps its identity as it dims: dark red is maroon, dark blue is navy. Dark yellow is olive or brown, which the eye files under dirt and warning. Straw sits at 97°, on the green side of yellow, so any low-alpha straw over black lands in olive. The revision saw khaki and removed chroma, which produced fog. The fix is hue, not saturation. Move to 65° to 75°, the amber of tungsten and candle flame, and the dimmed states become warm brown instead of olive.
  • Alpha is paint, screen is light. Normal alpha blending interpolates toward the tint, so a 20% amber over basalt is a muddy mid-tone. Screen blending only adds luminance, which is what a lamp does. The same amber at screen never passes through mud on its way to dark.
  • Dark surrounds drain color. On a dark ground the eye adapts and perceived colorfulness drops, the Hunt effect. Light on dark needs more chroma than pigment on paper, not less. That is why the light theme's 32% straw works while the dark theme's 10% warm white does not.

Why basalt stays blue

The ground at 248° is cool, and the first instinct is to warm it so the lamp stops fighting. Do not. Cool shadow against warm light is chiaroscuro, the oldest and most legible depth cue in painting, and it is why a lamp on a night desk reads as a lamp at all. A warm ground under a warm lamp is a sepia photograph. Keep basalt at 248° and let the surfaces near a lamp pick up a trace of it, the way a desk does.

4 · The Explain card

A question is not a claim

Decision. Question and answer stay together on the surface. The provenance rule is narrowed to what it was always about: authorship of knowledge. Requests, labels, and navigation are exempt.

You are right and the rule was over-reaching. Provenance exists so the reader can never mistake the AI's facts for their own insight. A question is neither. It is the title of the answer, and splitting the pair to satisfy a rule would trade a real cognitive unit for a doctrinal one. The rule now reads: surface holds what the AI asserts, ground holds what the human asserts, and a question belongs to whichever it heads.

One refinement worth keeping from the split: the question is set in the reading face, italic, in your ink, still inside the card. It reads as your voice opening the exchange without leaving the unit.

5 · Reaction state

Composition, not completion

Decision. Kill the meter. A fraction of paragraphs reacted to rewards reacting to everything, which is the highlighter habit wearing a bar. Replace it with the paper's spine: a thin line that shows where you reacted and, more importantly, whether you asked or said.

The provocation the posture promises is seeing your own pattern. The pattern that matters is not how much of a paper you touched but what kind of touch it was. A paper with twelve Explains and no Notes is a paper you consumed. The spine draws that without a number: ticks on the left are questions the AI answered, ticks on the right are things you said in your own words. A left-heavy spine is the tool's only critique of you, and it never says a word.

The same object works at three sizes. In the sidebar it is 22px tall beside the row. In the reader it is the scroll track, so the gutter you write in is also the map of where you have written. In the Workbench it is the project, one spine per paper, stacked by map layer.

6 · Workbench and motion

The Workbench is the ladder

Decision. Drop Inbox and Thinking. The tabs are the rungs: Reactions · Insights · Theses · Playbook, each with its count. Every rung shows the rung below it as material on the left and a place to write on the right.

"Thinking" fails the new-user test because it names a mental state, not a thing on screen, and "Inbox" imports email's guilt. The user's actual problem is synthesis under a weekly deadline: a pile of reactions has to become a few insights and one or two defended theses. Synthesis tools fail when they hand over a blank page. This product's answer is already written in its posture: render the state and let seeing provoke. So the Reactions rung is not a list, it is your reactions grouped by map layer with the asked-versus-said composition drawn per layer. A layer where you asked nine times and said nothing is an insight waiting for a sentence, and the field to write it is beside the evidence, not in another tab.

7 · Accessibility

Pen and wash

Decision. Pastel is never asked to be a line or a letter on paper. On light, every hue appears as a wash under dark ink, and where a line or glyph must carry the hue it is the same pigment concentrated. On dark, pastel is ink and passes on its own. Every pair in the system clears WCAG 2.2 AA.

This is the eighteenth-century watercolorist's method, dilute pigment for areas and the same pigment loaded for lines, and it is also the only way pastels survive a contrast audit. A pale teal cannot reach 4.5:1 on warm paper at any size; the arithmetic forbids it. But ink over a pale teal wash reaches 13:1, and a loaded teal line reaches 3.7:1, which is the floor for a graphic that carries meaning. Nothing in the aesthetic is given up. The hue is just used as an area, and lines are drawn in the concentrated form.

Beyond contrast

  • Use of color. No meaning rides on hue alone. Human writing is outdent plus rule plus typeface. Asked versus said on the spine is left versus right. Counter open versus resolved is outline versus fill. Hover is fill plus underline. Warm versus cold is therefore ornament on top of structure, which is what lets it be pastel.
  • Target size. Edge-strip dots, margin markers, citations, and spine ticks are drawn small and hit large: a 24px minimum target around each, per WCAG 2.2.
  • Names for the marks. Every drawn-only object carries its meaning in text for a screen reader: the spine announces "5 asked, 0 said," a margin marker announces its type and state, the edge strip announces "2 deep, layer 2 of 2."
  • Focus and hover are different states. The focus ring is always ink, the hover is always cold, so a keyboard user never loses focus inside a colored fill.
  • Motion. Reduced motion cuts every animation to its end state, including the 900ms rewards.
  • Type size. Nothing readable sits under 12px. The mono coordinate register moves from 11 to 12.
Decide

Five calls, in the order they unblock the rest

  1. Accent. Cold teal at 198°: ink at lightness .78 on dark, on light a .92 wash under ink and a .60 line, for everything the interface offers; warm only for what you made. Recommended: yes, and verdigris's tokens are renamed and re-valued rather than added to.
  2. Lamp. Amber near 70° at screen blend on cool basalt. Recommended: yes, and I re-render artboard 03's three brightnesses and the Brief with it.
  3. Human writing. Outdent plus warm rule, both themes; on light the rule is sepia, the loaded form of the straw. Recommended: yes. React to the sepia line specifically: it is the one place the accessibility floor changed a color you had approved.
  4. Spine. Replace the meter with the asked-versus-said spine at three sizes. Recommended: yes. This one changes the data model: reactions need a paragraph anchor, which they already have.
  5. Workbench. Reactions · Insights · Theses · Playbook, evidence left, writing right. Recommended: yes, and "Reactions" is the name I would test first on a new user, against "Notes."

Section 3's lamp remains warm amber. The cold is an ink, never a glow, so the two families never compete for the same physics. Once these land I write prompt 1c as a single revision to the canvas, then prompt 2 for the screens with the approved sheet pasted in.
