## About the example

`04` is a real, complete topic/pedagogy BrainLift: **Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs)**, written by Becky Allen for item writers and curriculum leads. It's one of the strongest examples we have — in fact, our internal "Anatomy of a BrainLift" reference draws two of its textbook illustrations straight from this document (the SPOV "MCQs are learning events first" and the Insight "Wrong answers are diagnostic, not random"). It's included unedited so you can see what "finished and load-bearing" actually looks like.

Two things to know before you read it:

- **Formatting.** This one is written as a nested outline (it was imported from another tool), so sections are bold bullet headings — `**Purpose**`, `**DOK4 — Spiky Points of View**`, `**SPOV 1: …**` — rather than `##` markdown headings. The *structure* is exactly the structure taught in `02`; only the visual formatting differs. Don't let the outline throw you.
- **It's long (~15,000 words).** That's the size a mature, heavily-iterated BrainLift reaches after many passes. **Do not expect your first one to look like this** — start with short daily notes (`05`). For this read, you don't need every word: follow the section-by-section guide below and read those parts closely.

## Read it in this order

Read a strong BrainLift top-down for the *claims*, then bottom-up to *check the trail*. Here's what to look for in each section.

### 1. The Purpose does real work
Read the **Purpose** and its **In scope / Out of scope** lists. Notice how specific the audience is ("item writers, curriculum leads, and anyone who commissions, reviews, or gives feedback on MCQ-based assessments") and the one commitment the whole document rests on: **MCQs are treated as both measurement instruments *and* learning events.** That dual claim is the seed every SPOV grows from.

Look at **Out of scope**: it pushes away advanced psychometrics (IRT, adaptive testing), non-MCQ formats, and implementation — each with a pointer to where that lives. That boundary is what keeps a 15,000-word document from becoming 50,000. 

**Steal this:** before writing anything else, write a Purpose so specific it can *reject* material. If you can't tell whether a fact belongs, your Purpose is too vague.

### 2. The SPOVs are genuinely spiky
Go to **SPOV 5 — "'All of the above' and 'None of the above' should never be used."** It's a great first SPOV to study because it's so crisp you could argue it over coffee: it names the default it fights ("these formats are common and feel harmless"), gives the mechanism (AOTA is answerable with partial knowledge; NOTA imposes an asymmetric cognitive task), and commits hard ("There is no assessment context in which they improve measurement").

Then read **SPOV 1 — "MCQs are learning events first and measurement events second."** This is the document's central inversion. Watch how it works:

- **Claim** — one sentence that flips the industry's default (design for measurement first).
- **Why it's contrarian** — "almost all MCQ design guidance treats items purely as measurement instruments."
- **Evidence** — a specific number: the testing effect for MCQ practice is g = +0.70 vs. restudying, "one of the largest effect sizes in educational psychology."
- **Operational consequences** — design for retrieval first (strong cues, competitive distractors, immediate feedback).

Now find the SPOV that carries a **falsifiable test** most explicitly and read how the author states, in advance, what evidence would weaken the claim. That "I'd be wrong if ___" move is the difference between a BrainLift and an opinion piece.

**Steal this:** for every position you take, write the sentence "I would abandon this if ______." If you can't, you don't have an SPOV yet — you have a belief.

### 3. Notice how Insights differ from SPOVs
Jump to **Insight 7 — "Wrong answers are often diagnostic, not random."** This isn't contrarian; once said, most educators nod. But it's *non-obvious before it's said*, and it yields a concrete rule (a specific distractor a student picks reveals which wrong schema they activated). That's the signature of a DOK3 Insight versus a DOK4 SPOV. Contrast it with **Insight 5 — "Difficulty and cognitive level are independent"** — again a rule you *derive and apply*, not a position you have to defend. The SPOVs make you argue; the Insights make you nod. That contrast is the tiering working correctly.

### 4. The Blueprints are executable
Read **Blueprint 1 — Item Writing Checklist.** It's a real workflow a newcomer could run, and every judgment call is annotated with the tier it rests on — e.g. "*MCQ or constructed response?* … Default to short constructed-response for calculation-heavy items `(KT5, Insight 6)`." Ask yourself the Blueprint test: could a competent newcomer, using only this, actually write and vet an item? Notice nothing floats — each step points back down to a KT branch or an Insight.

### 5. The Knowledge Tree is the backbone everything hangs on
Skim the **9 KT branches** (KT1 stem construction → KT9 what inferences you can draw). Each follows the same internal shape and is numbered. Now go back up to any SPOV or Blueprint step and notice the inline tags — `[Readings 7, 8, 10, 22]`, `(KT2, Insight 2)`. That's the traceability. **Try it:** take SPOV 2 ("distractor quality and feedback quality are coupled"), find the KT branch it rests on (KT7, "what should happen after a student answers"), and follow that down to the source in DOK1 (Butler & Roediger, 2008). It should take under a minute. That traversal *is* the BrainLift working.

### 6. DOK1 is honest about its own weaknesses
Go to **"Key Facts and Relevance by Paper."** Each source has three parts: **Key facts** (extracted findings and numbers, not an abstract), **Limitations**, and **Relevance to Alpha** (which even lists the KT branches it covers). Read the **Limitations** lines — "Primarily drawn from medical and higher education contexts," "some guidelines rest on expert consensus rather than strong empirical evidence." A weaker document hides its soft spots; this one lists them next to the facts it's leaning on. That honesty is exactly what makes the confident SPOVs above it *credible*.

### 7. It names its own central tension
The whole document hinges on a genuine tension it refuses to paper over: competitive, misconception-based distractors are the *best* tool for diagnosis and deep retrieval — and the *most dangerous* for implanting false knowledge (the "negative suggestion effect"). Rather than pretend one side wins, the BrainLift resolves it into a rule (**SPOV 2**: distractor quality and feedback quality are coupled decisions — you can't set one without the other). A BrainLift that names and *resolves* a real tension is doing the thing a wiki page never does.

## What makes this one good — the short list

- A **Purpose sharp enough to reject** irrelevant material, built on one clear commitment.
- **6 SPOVs**, each with a named contrarian default, specific evidence, and a falsifiable test.
- **Insights that are rules, not restatements** of the sources.
- **Blueprints a newcomer could execute**, each grounded in lower tiers.
- **Full traceability** — inline tags let you walk any claim down to a citation.
- **Honest DOK1** — every source's limitations sit right next to its facts.
- **A named owner** and a real tension the document resolves rather than hides.
